>[!info] Source  Note
>Sylvia Mathews Burwell, “Generation Stress: The Mental Health Crisis on Campus.” They Say / I Say: the Moves That Matter in Academic Writing With Readings, edited by Gerald Graff, Cathy Birkenstein, Russel Durst, and Laura J. Panning Davies, W. W. Norton & Company, 2024, pp. 578 - 587.

- [ ] Work backwards from conclusion.

In Burwell's essay, she makes the case that universities should take action on today's mental health crisis. She begins presenting evidence that there is a problem, showing rising indicators of depression and anxiety among students (578). She then presents an analysis of a poll she conducted about the concerns of students at her university (580) and also sets it apart from previous generations (580, 581-583). Finally she concludes with a list of policies to adopt (585-586).

She first argues that there is a mental health crisis (578), building her case that universities must, in the manner they built the middle class, act for the prosperity of the nation during a time of need (583). She supports this with In a then-recent survey, 39 percent of students reported experiencing depression or anxiety. On top of this, suicidal ideation is rising among that population (578). Even among non-students, she points out, there are issues, such as the increase in completed suicide among all adults (578).

During her time as President of American University, she gathered insights from students and discovered three factors that work contrary to the responsibilities of universities: safety, economics, and technology (580). As for safety, students experience both theoretical and existential threats. Students have experienced their whole life with the post-9/11 increase in police and military surveillance along with the knowledge an attack could happen again (580). There are also on-campus threats that include racially motivated violence and sexual assault (580). In terms of economics, while colleges should accelerate social mobility, tuition is rising, the job market is daunting, and there is a lack of wage growth. Lastly, technology such as social media requires constant performance, likening it to professional development by way of a parallel with resume building (581).

She argues that a lack of resilience in students means that stress is felt more than ever (582). She directs the blame at over protective parents who, she claims, created a culture where risk was not tolerated (582). She calls on universities to help change this culture (587).

As for action, she points towards education, resourcing, community building, and a culture shift. For education, emphasis is placed on teaching both teachers and students about prevention, recognition, and response. This reflects the three priorities of controlling the 2014 Ebola outbreak, which she states universities should take inspiration from (586). She also states that universities need to increase resources for counseling and suggests establishing a triage system to cope with the inevitable overflow (586). She argues that universities should build inclusive communities, alluding to studies that have been conducted (586). She concludes with celebrating healthy stress and encourages students, parents, and universities should aspire to maintain it (586).
